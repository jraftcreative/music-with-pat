A mother emailed me last week about her six-year-old son's Grade 1 ABRSM result. He'd passed — just. She wasn't writing because she was disappointed in the mark. She was writing because, two months after the exam, he refused to sit at the piano. He cried when she opened the lid. The certificate was on the fridge and the love of playing was gone. The thing she wanted to ask me was simple: did the exam break him, or had something been broken long before?

It was the second one. And it's one of the most common stories I hear at this age.

## What "Most Studios" Actually Do With a Five-Year-Old

In Singapore, the route looks the same almost everywhere. A family signs up at a nearby studio when the child turns five. The first lesson — sometimes the very first — opens a method book on the stand. Middle C. Right-hand thumb. Quarter notes. Within a few weeks the child is reading off a page. Within six months, the studio quietly mentions the ABRSM Prep Test, or skips straight to Grade 1 entry by age six or seven. Three pieces, scales, sight-reading, aural — all packed into a child who has only just learned to tie her own shoes.

Behind the scenes, the structure is almost always the same. A standardised curriculum the teacher didn't write. A pacing chart that assumes every five-year-old learns in the same order at the same speed. A natural pull toward exam entries, because exam fees are a clean way to show "progress" to a paying parent. And often a teacher who is a fine pianist but has never been trained in how four- and five-year-olds actually learn.

I don't think any of this is malicious. Most teachers genuinely want the child to do well. But the system is built for throughput, not for childhood. And in Singapore, where parents are already running an enrichment-class arms race from K1, the pressure to move fast is louder than the small voice asking whether the child is actually ready.

The result, in my experience, is a generation of young pianists who can read notes before they can listen to music. That's the wrong way round.

## Why This Breaks Young Kids

Between ages four and six, a child's brain is doing something specific. It is wiring the auditory system — pitch discrimination, rhythm perception, melodic memory, the ability to hear a phrase as a phrase rather than a string of separate sounds. This is a foundational window, and it doesn't reopen later in the same way. The research on this is decades deep, and it's the entire reason early-childhood music education exists as a discipline. It's also half of what my B.Ed in Early Childhood with Music Education was actually about.

When a teacher puts a four- or five-year-old straight into note-reading and exam repertoire, two things happen. The child learns to look at the page first and listen to the sound second — which sounds backwards because it is. And the natural musical ear, the thing that would have grown effortlessly with the right input at the right age, gets quietly skipped over. The child can pass exams for years before anyone notices the gap. In my studio I usually see it surface around Grade 4 or 5, when the music starts asking for musicality and there isn't any to draw on.

The exam pressure compounds it. A young child's relationship with the piano is fragile at the start. It is built lesson by lesson, in moments where they feel capable and curious and a little bit proud. When that early relationship gets forced into a performance frame — assessed pieces, marked components, a stranger in a room with a clipboard — before the child has the emotional or technical maturity to hold it, the wiring takes. They learn that piano is something you can fail at. That feeling is very hard to undo at six.

This is why my training in early-childhood pedagogy is the part of my background I lean on most when I teach young children. The pedagogy for a four-year-old is not "Grade 1 piano, just slower." It is its own discipline, with its own sequence and its own rules. Skipping it is the quiet error underneath most of the broken Grade 1 stories that land in my inbox.

## What I Do Differently

The first six to twelve months with a young child in my studio usually contain very little of what a parent might recognise as "real piano." There is keyboard work, but it's built on top of listening, singing, and rhythm — not the other way round. A typical early lesson with me looks like:

- Call-and-response singing to train pitch matching.
- Body-percussion games to internalise rhythm before reading it.
- Short improvisations on a small group of black keys, so the child experiences making music before being asked to read it.
- Short pieces by ear, and then from notation as I introduce it gradually.

I do teach notation. I just teach it second, not first. A child learns the sound of "step up, step down" before learning the symbol for it. They feel a steady beat in their body before counting it in their head. By the time staff notation arrives, the child isn't decoding a foreign language — they're putting names to sounds they already know. This is the heart of the [piano lessons designed for young children](/childrens-piano-lessons/) I run.

I'm also fussy about repertoire for young hands, because a lot of curricula get this wrong. Five-year-old fingers are short, the muscles between thumb and palm are still developing, and the hand can comfortably reach about a fifth, not an octave. Pieces that require stretching or fast passagework at this age don't build technique — they build tension and bad habits that take years for me to undo later. So I choose repertoire the small hand can play beautifully, and I let technical range expand as the body grows.

ABRSM enters the picture when I think the child is ready, and not before. For many of my young students, that means starting with the Prep Test around age six or seven — a gentle, mark-free introduction designed for exactly this purpose. *(My understanding is the Prep Test is mark-free rather than graded — worth a final check with the latest ABRSM materials before publish.)* For others, it means waiting until seven or eight to enter Grade 1 with playing that's genuinely musical, not just technically accurate. And there are children for whom exams are simply the wrong fit for now — and that's a real answer too, one I'm willing to give parents. The exam is a tool. It should serve the child, not the other way round. When the time is right, I move them into [structured ABRSM piano preparation](/abrsm-piano-lessons-singapore/) at a pace that fits the child.

What this looks like from the outside is a slower-feeling first year and a much stronger second, third, and fourth. The children I build up properly tend to enjoy Grade 1 rather than survive it — and most are still happily playing at Grade 5, when the children who were rushed are quietly dropping out.

## But Will My Child Still Pass Exams?

This is the question that lives under every other question, and it deserves a direct answer. Yes. My students sit ABRSM exams and they pass them — usually with Merit or Distinction, because preparation built on a strong ear and secure technique tends to translate into a better mark, not a worse one. Examiners listen for musicality alongside accuracy, and a child who has been trained to listen has an audible advantage in the room. If you want the practical side of preparing for the day itself, I've written separately on [how to prepare for an ABRSM piano exam](/blog/how-to-prepare-abrsm-piano-exam-singapore/).

What changes with my approach is the timeline and the entry point, not the destination. A child who starts piano at five with me might sit the Prep Test at six or seven and Grade 1 at seven or eight, instead of being pushed into Grade 1 at six. That difference of a year or two is invisible on the certificate. It is hugely visible in how the child plays — and in whether they're still playing at twelve.

ABRSM itself recommends that children be entered for grades when they are technically and musically ready, not at a fixed age. The board doesn't reward earlier entry. There is no advantage on the certificate, in school admissions, or in DSA applications to a Grade 1 pass at six over a Grade 2 pass at eight. What does help, in every one of those settings, is a child who plays with confidence and obvious musicianship. That is what I'm trying to build.

The honest version of this conversation is that a child rushed into Grade 1 too early may pass — and lose interest, or arrive at higher grades with foundations that won't hold. A child I prepare properly may enter slightly later, and keep playing for the rest of their life. I'd rather build the second outcome.

## How to Know If Your Child Is Ready for ABRSM Grade 1

A few clear signs I look for when I'm deciding whether a young child is genuinely ready to enter Grade 1:

- They can play their three pieces comfortably on an average day, not just on their best day.
- They can play scales and arpeggios from memory without major hesitation.
- They can read a short, unfamiliar piece slowly and steadily — not perfectly, but without freezing.
- They can perform for a family member in the living room and stay together when something goes slightly wrong.
- They still enjoy sitting at the piano, and they're entering the exam because they want to, not because the calendar says it's time.

If three or four of these are in place, the child is in a good window. If they're not, I usually recommend waiting six more months. It almost always produces a better result — and a child who still loves the instrument afterwards.
