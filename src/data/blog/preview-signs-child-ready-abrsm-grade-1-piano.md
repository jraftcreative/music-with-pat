So here's the honest answer, from someone who sits next to small children at the piano every week.

## When is a child ready for ABRSM Grade 1 piano?

A child is ready for ABRSM Grade 1 piano when they can play short pieces with both hands together, read notes on the stave, keep a steady beat, and sit calmly through a few minutes of focused playing for someone they don't know. Age isn't the test — readiness is. Some children are there at six, some at eight, and both are perfectly normal.

I look at it this way partly because of my training. My degree is in Early Childhood Education with Music Education, so when I'm deciding whether a child is ready for their first exam, I'm reading how they actually learn and cope — not just how many notes they can play. Here are the five signs I look for.

## Sign 1: They can play short pieces with both hands together

**If both hands can hold their own at the same time, a real part of the Grade 1 puzzle is already in place.**

Grade 1 asks for three pieces, played start to finish. That takes hands that can cooperate — the left doing one thing while the right does another, without one of them quietly giving up. A child who can only play single-hand tunes, or who stalls the moment the left hand joins in, usually needs a bit more time at the keyboard first. That's not a problem. It's just the foundation still setting.

## Sign 2: They read notes on the stave, not just finger numbers

**Real reading — not memorising which finger goes where — is what makes everything after Grade 1 possible.**

Plenty of young children learn their first pieces by finger number or by ear, and that's a lovely start. But Grade 1 includes sight-reading: a short piece the child has never seen, played then and there. For that, they need to actually read the dots on the stave. If your child still relies on "1-2-3" written under the notes, they're not quite ready — and pushing the exam before the reading is solid tends to bake in a habit we later have to undo.

## Sign 3: They can keep a steady beat and copy back a rhythm

**A child who can clap back a rhythm and hold a steady pulse is ready for the aural tests — and sounds musical doing everything else.**

The Grade 1 aural tests ask a child to keep time, clap back rhythms, and respond to what they hear. Beyond the exam, a steady beat is what separates playing that sounds musical from playing that stumbles. I test this without the child even realising — I'll tap a rhythm and ask them to echo it, or play while they march. If they can hold the pulse and bounce a rhythm back to me, that box is ticked.

## Sign 4: They can practise most days and focus through a full lesson

**Exam prep rewards steady habits, not raw talent — so the quiet skill of showing up daily matters more than you'd think.**

Preparing for Grade 1 means revisiting the same pieces, scales and arpeggios over several months. That needs a child who can manage a little practice most days and stay with a 20-to-30-minute lesson without melting down. I'd rather delay the exam for a child who practises happily three minutes a day than rush one who can technically play but dreads sitting down. The habit outlasts the certificate.

## Sign 5: They can perform for someone without falling apart

**The exam is a solo performance for a stranger in a quiet room — so emotional readiness counts as much as the playing.**

This is the sign most people forget. On exam day your child walks into a room, alone, and plays for an examiner they've never met. A child who clams up or cries when anyone watches isn't being difficult — they're just telling you they're not ready for that part yet. I build this gently over time: playing for me, then for a parent, then for a small group. When a child can perform without it rattling them, they're ready for the room.

## What if your child isn't ready yet?

Then you wait, and you lose nothing.

This is the honest bit, and it's the thing I feel most strongly about. In Singapore there's real pressure to collect grades early, and yes — plenty of five- and six-year-olds *can* be drilled through Grade 1. The question I'd ask instead is whether they come out the other side still loving it. Far fewer do.

My approach is foundation first: solid reading, a steady beat, real enjoyment, and exams only when the child is genuinely ready. Grades are a useful checkpoint, not the point. A child who sits Grade 1 a year "late" but still wants to play at twelve has won something a rushed distinction can't buy. ## Frequently asked questions

### What age is ABRSM Grade 1 piano for?

There's no set age. ABRSM doesn't impose a minimum, and children sit Grade 1 anywhere from around six to nine, depending on when they started and how they've developed. Readiness — reading, coordination, a steady beat, and the nerve to perform — matters far more than the number on the birthday cake.

### How long does it take to prepare for ABRSM Grade 1?

For a child with a solid foundation, preparing the three pieces, scales, sight-reading and aural usually takes a few months of steady, happy practice — not a frantic cram. The timeline depends on how secure the basics already are. If the fundamentals aren't there yet, it's better to build those first than to rush the exam.

### Is my child too young for Grade 1?

Probably not "too young" by age — but possibly not ready yet, which is different. A young child who reads music, plays hands together, keeps a beat and performs comfortably is ready regardless of age. One who's still learning those things simply needs more time at the keyboard first, and that's completely normal.
