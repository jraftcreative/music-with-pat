A dad stood in my studio doorway a few weeks ago while his four-year-old poked at the keys, and asked me the question I wish more parents asked out loud: *"Ms Pat, how do I even know what a good teacher looks like? They all say the same things on their websites."*

He's right. They do. Everyone promises "fun, patient, qualified." The words are free.

So here's the honest version, from someone on the other side of the piano. For a young child, the right teacher isn't the one with the highest playing grade or the longest list of competition wins. It's the one who understands how a four-, five-, or six-year-old actually learns — who can hold a small child's attention, build their confidence, and keep them wanting to come back. Everything else is secondary to that.

Here's what to actually look for.

## 1. Training in how young children learn — not just how to play

**A Grade 8 pianist is not automatically good with a five-year-old.** Those are two completely different skills.

Playing beautifully is about your own hands. Teaching a young child is about *their* brain — how long they can focus, how they take in new information, when to push and when to back off before a small person shuts down. A teacher can have a diploma and still lose a room full of preschoolers in ten minutes.

This is the part I care about most, and I'll be upfront about why: my own degree is in Early Childhood Education with Music Education. So when I sit next to a four-year-old, I'm not just teaching notes — I'm working with what I know about how children that age develop attention, memory, and fine motor control. You don't need every teacher to have that exact qualification. But you do want someone who can talk about *children*, not just about music. Ask them about it and listen to whether the answer is about your child or about the syllabus.

## 2. Real experience with young children specifically

**Ask how much of their week is actually spent teaching children your child's age.**

Some teachers are wonderful with teenagers doing Grade 6 and above, and genuinely out of their depth with a wriggly five-year-old. That's not a flaw — it's a specialism. You just want to match it to your child.

A teacher who works with young beginners every day has seen it all: the child who cries at the first wrong note, the one who wants to play only the black keys, the one who needs to march around the room once before they'll sit. None of that rattles them, because it's Tuesday.

## 3. Patience and play — not pressure

**Watch how they respond when your child gets something wrong.** That single moment tells you more than any certificate.

Young children learn through play, repetition, and feeling safe. A good early-years lesson often doesn't look like a "lesson" at all — there are games, silly songs, clapping, a bit of moving around. That's not the teacher being soft. That's the teacher meeting a five-year-old where their attention span actually lives.

The warning sign is pressure: a teacher who's visibly impatient, who drills a nervous child, who makes a small person feel they've disappointed someone. Music should never become the thing your child dreads on a Wednesday afternoon.

## 4. One-to-one attention, at least for the first few years

**For a young beginner, individual attention beats the big group class.**

I know the chain music schools are convenient, and group classes have their place later on. But in the early years, when a child is forming their very first relationship with an instrument, they need someone watching *their* hands, correcting *their* posture, noticing the exact moment they're about to give up. In a group of eight, that gets diluted.

One child, one teacher, full attention. That's how the foundation gets built properly the first time — so nobody has to un-teach bad habits down the road.

## 5. Structure — without the grade race

**Look for a clear path, but run from anyone who wants to rush your four-year-old towards an exam.**

This is a real tension in Singapore, so let me say it plainly. Yes, you want a teacher with structure — a sense of where your child is heading, not just aimless noodling. But you do *not* want a teacher who treats a young beginner as an ABRSM Grade 1 result waiting to happen.

Plenty of small children *can* be pushed through an early grade. Far fewer come out the other side still loving it. My approach is foundation first — solid reading, steady rhythm, real enjoyment — and exams later, if and when the child is genuinely ready. Grades are a tool, not the point. A good teacher will tell you the same. ## 6. The practical things parents forget to check

**The best teacher in Singapore is no use to you if the logistics don't work.**

Before you commit, sort out the boring-but-important stuff:

- **Lesson length.** Thirty minutes is plenty for most young children. An hour-long lesson for a four-year-old usually means twenty minutes of learning and forty of fidgeting.
- **Location.** Do they teach at their studio, or can they come to you? I teach from my home studio in Tengah and also do home visits around the west — [Bukit Batok, Jurong, Choa Chu Kang and nearby](https://www.musicwithpat.com/piano-lessons-bukit-batok/) — because a child who's just been dragged across the island is not in a learning mood.
- **Parent communication.** Will they tell you what to do between lessons? The child who practises a little most days, with a parent gently in the loop, always outpaces the one who only touches the piano once a week.

## The questions I'd ask before booking any teacher

If you take nothing else from this, take these. Message or call a prospective teacher and ask:

1. **Have you taught children as young as mine, and what does a typical first lesson look like?**
2. **How do you keep a young child engaged when their focus starts to drift?**
3. **How do you decide when a child is ready for exams — and what if I'd rather not rush that?**
4. **How long are your lessons for young children, and do you teach at your studio or at home?**
5. **How will I know my child is making progress in the first few months?**
6. **What do you need me, as a parent, to do between lessons?**

You'll learn everything you need to from *how* they answer. A teacher who lights up talking about young children is a very different prospect from one who reels off their own credentials.
